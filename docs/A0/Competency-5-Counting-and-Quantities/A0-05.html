Lesson A0-05: Counting and Basic Quantities ​

Level: A0 (Foundations) CEFR Level: A0 Duration: 75 minutes New Vocabulary: 60 words Grammar Focus: Cardinal numbers (0--100), ordinals (1st--10th), "Hoeveel?" questions, plural forms, number agreement, monetary expressions

Lesson Goal ​

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to count from 0 to 100, use numbers with plural nouns, ask "how many?" and "how much?", recognize and use ordinal numbers (first through tenth), and understand basic prices in euros and cents.

Previous Knowledge ​

Before starting this lesson, you should know:

  • Personal pronouns (ik, jij, hij, zij, wij, etc.) from A0-02
  • The verbs hebben (to have) and zijn (to be) from A0-02 and A0-03
  • Demonstratives (dit, dat) from A0-03
  • Simple sentence structure (subject + verb + object) from A0-02
  • Basic adjectives and nouns from A0-01

If you're reviewing: Make sure you can recognize and use present tense of "hebben" and "zijn" before beginning.

Why This Matters ​

Numbers are everywhere in daily life. Whether you're shopping at a Dutch supermarket, asking someone's age, reading a house address, checking the time, or understanding prices, you need to count fluently in Dutch. This lesson also teaches you how to ask "how much?" and "how many?" -- the most practical questions for everyday communication. You'll learn that Dutch numbers have a unique reversed structure from 21--99 (e.g., "vierentwintig" = four-and-twenty = 24), which feels strange at first but becomes automatic with practice.

Grammar ​

Compound Numbers (21--99):

From 21--99, reverse the order: unit + en + tens

  • vierentwintig = four-and-twenty = 24
  • tweeëntwintig = two-and-twenty = 22
  • drieëndertig = three-and-thirty = 33
  • vierendertig = four-and-thirty = 34
  • vijfenvijftig = five-and-fifty = 55
  • negenennegentig = ninety-nine = 99

2. "Hoeveel?" (How Many? / How Much?) ​

"Hoeveel?" is a question word that means "how many?" or "how much?" In Dutch questions, the verb comes immediately after the question word:

QuestionAnswer
Hoeveel appels heb je?Ik heb vijf appels.
Hoeveel kost het?Dit kost vier euro.
Hoeveel boeken zijn er?Er zijn tien boeken.
Hoeveel kinderen heb je?Ik heb drie kinderen.

Compare with yes/no questions, where the verb comes first:

  • Heb je vijf appels? (Do you have five apples?)
  • Kost het vier euro? (Does it cost four euros?)

4. Plural Nouns with Numbers ​

After any number greater than 1, Dutch uses plural nouns. Most plurals end in --en or --s:

SingularPluralExample
een appeltwee appelsTwo apples
een katdrie kattenThree cats
een boekvijf boekenFive books
een kindtwee kinderenTwo children
een tafelvier tafelsFour tables

Important rule: All plural nouns use the article "de" (even if the singular uses "het"):

  • het boek (singular) -- de boeken (plural)
  • het huis (singular) -- de huizen (plural)

5. Number--Noun Agreement ​

Dutch numbers themselves do NOT change. The noun changes form for plural:

  • één boek (one book) / twee boeken (two books)
  • één kat (one cat) / drie katten (three cats)

Use één (with accent) when you want to emphasize "one" specifically. Use een (without accent) for "a/an" or unstressed "one":

  • Ik heb één boek. (I have ONE book -- emphasis on the number)
  • Ik heb een boek. (I have a book)

6. Basic Monetary Expressions ​

The Netherlands uses the euro (€), divided into 100 cent. Prices are written with a comma as the decimal separator: €4,50 (four euros fifty cents).

  • €10,00 = tien euro

Grammar Reasoning ​

Why Dutch Reverses Numbers 21--99 ​

English says "twenty-four" (tens-then-units), but Dutch says "vierentwintig" (units-then-tens). This feels backwards to English speakers, but it's consistent with Dutch's emphasis on meaningful order: you say the thing you're counting (the unit) before its category (the group of tens). This is also found in German, which uses the same reversed structure.

Why "Hoeveel" Changes Sentence Word Order ​

In Dutch, question words (Hoeveel, Wat, Wie, etc.) trigger "V2" word order: the conjugated verb comes immediately after the question word, before the subject.

  • Hoeveel appels heb je? (How many apples have you?)
  • Compare: Jij hebt appels. (You have apples -- normal statement order)

English does the same thing:

  • How many apples do you have? (V2: verb after question word)
  • Compare: You have apples. (normal order)

Why "Honderd" Doesn't Take an Article ​

When counting exactly 100, Dutch says "honderd" (not "een honderd" = one hundred). But when using "honderd" as a noun in other contexts, it can take an article: "de honderd" (the hundred, or "a/the number 100").

Pronunciation ​

Cardinal Number Stress ​

In compound numbers, stress falls on the first meaningful part:

  • VIJf-en-vij-tig (fifty-five) -- stress on first "vijf"
  • DRIE-en-der-tig (thirty-three) -- stress on "drie"
  • TWEE-en-twin-tig (twenty-two) -- stress on "twee"

The "g" and "ch" in Larger Numbers ​

In "acht," "tachtig," and "negentig," the "g/ch" is a guttural sound from deep in the throat, like a rough "kh" sound. This is distinctly Dutch and takes practice.

  • acht = AHT (with throat sound)
  • tachtig = TAHKH-tigh (throat sound on both syllables)

Diaeresis (ë, ï, etc.) ​

When Dutch has a vowel collision in compound numbers, it uses a diaeresis mark (two dots) to show they're pronounced as separate vowels:

  • tweeëntwintig (two--and--twenty): "twee" + "en" + "twintig" = three separate vowel sounds
  • drieëndertig (three--and--thirty): "drie" + "en" + "dertig" = three separate vowel sounds

This is a spelling convention only; pronunciation doesn't change.

Dialogue ​

Dialogue 1: Counting Objects ​

Dutch:

A: Hoeveel appels heb je? B: Ik heb vijf appels. A: En hoeveel boeken? B: Twee boeken.

Translation:

A: How many apples do you have? B: I have five apples. A: And how many books? B: Two books.

Dialogue 2: Asking Prices ​

Dutch:

A: Hoeveel kost dit brood? B: Dit kost twee euro vijftig. A: Oké. Ik betaal met pin. B: Prima.

Translation:

A: How much does this bread cost? B: This costs two euros fifty. A: Okay. I'll pay by card. B: Fine.

Dialogue 3: Quantities ​

Dutch:

A: Heb je veel melk? B: Nee, ik heb weinig melk. A: Ik heb een liter melk. Is dat genoeg? B: Ja, dat is genoeg.

Translation:

A: Do you have a lot of milk? B: No, I have little milk. A: I have one liter of milk. Is that enough? B: Yes, that is enough.

Dialogue 4: Ordinals ​

Dutch:

A: Wat is de eerste vraag? B: Hoeveel katten heb je? A: En de tweede vraag? B: Hoeveel kost het?

Translation:

A: What is the first question? B: How many cats do you have? A: And the second question? B: How much does it cost?

Dialogue 5: Age ​

Dutch:

A: Hoe oud ben je? B: Ik ben vijfentwintig jaar. A: Ik ben dertig jaar. B: Vandaag is de tiende.

Translation:

A: How old are you? B: I am twenty-five years old. A: I am thirty years old. B: Today is the tenth.

Reading ​

Short Text: Een Familie en Getallen (A Family and Numbers) ​

Ik ben Jan. Ik ben dertig jaar. Dit is Anna. Zij is negenentwintig jaar. We hebben vier katten. De eerste kat is rood. De tweede kat is zwart. De derde kat is wit. De vierde kat is grijs.

We hebben twee kinderen. Tom is acht jaar. Lisa is zes jaar. We wonen in een huis. Het huis kost veel geld!

In de keuken, we hebben negen borden, tien kopjes, en elf lepels. We hebben ook drie flessen melk en twee liters jus.

Elke dag, we drinken koffie. De koffie kost twee euro per kopje. Anna koopt ook brood. Het brood kost twee euro vijftig.

English Translation:

I am Jan. I am thirty years old. This is Anna. She is twenty-nine years old. We have four cats. The first cat is red. The second cat is black. The third cat is white. The fourth cat is gray.

We have two children. Tom is eight years old. Lisa is six years old. We live in a house. The house costs a lot of money!

In the kitchen, we have nine plates, ten cups, and eleven spoons. We also have three bottles of milk and two liters of juice.

Every day, we drink coffee. The coffee costs two euros per cup. Anna also buys bread. The bread costs two euros fifty.

Listening ​

Short Conversation: At a Café ​

Transcript:

A: Goedemorgen! Wat wil je? B: Ik wil een koffie en een brood, alstublieft. A: Hoeveel kopjes koffie? B: Eén kopje koffie. A: En wat voor brood? We hebben negen soorten. B: Ik wil het eerste brood. A: Prima. Dat is zes euro vijftig in totaal. B: Oké. Ik betaal met pin.

Translation:

A: Good morning! What would you like? B: I would like a coffee and bread, please. A: How many cups of coffee? B: One cup of coffee. A: And what kind of bread? We have nine types. B: I want the first bread. A: Fine. That's six euros fifty in total. B: Okay. I'll pay by card.

  1. Wat wil B drinken? (What does B want to drink?)
  2. Hoeveel brood types hebben ze? (How many types of bread do they have?)
  3. Welk brood wil B? (Which bread does B want?)
  4. Hoeveel kost het? (How much does it cost?)

Answers: 1. Koffie (coffee) 2. Negen (nine) 3. Het eerste (the first) 4. Zes euro vijftig (six euros fifty)

Common Mistakes ​

Mistake 1: English Number Order ​

Wrong: Twintig-vier (English order) Right: Vierentwintig (Dutch order: unit-and-tens) Why: Dutch reverses the number order from 21--99. Always say the small number first, then add "en," then the tens.

Mistake 2: Forgetting the "en" in Compound Numbers ​

Wrong: Vier twintig (missing the "en") Right: Vierentwintig (unit + en + tens) Why: The "en" is obligatory in written and spoken Dutch compound numbers.

Mistake 3: Forgetting Plural After Numbers > 1 ​

Wrong: Ik heb twee boek. (I have two book.) Right: Ik heb twee boeken. (I have two books.) Why: In Dutch, any noun after a number greater than 1 must be plural.

Mistake 4: Wrong Article with Plural ​

Wrong: Het boeken zijn groot. (The books are big -- using singular article with plural noun) Right: De boeken zijn groot. (The books are big -- using plural article) Why: All plural nouns use "de" as the article, regardless of singular gender (het or de).

Mistake 5: Mispronouncing "acht/tachtig/negentig" ​

Wrong: Pronounced like English "at" + "ch" Right: Pronounced with a guttural throat sound: AHT / TAHKH-tigh / NAY-ghen-tigh Why: Dutch "g" and "ch" in these words are back-of-throat sounds, not English "ck" sounds.

Mistake 6: Confusing "een" with "één" ​

Wrong: Ik heb een boeken. (I have one books -- mixing singular and plural) Right: Ik heb één boek. (I have one book -- singular) OR Ik heb één boeken niet. (literally: one books not -- avoiding the issue) Why: "Een" is unstressed (means "a" or unstressed "one"), while "één" (with accent) emphasizes the number one specifically. After "één," use singular; after "twee" or higher, use plural.

Dutch Insight ​

Dutch Number Culture ​

Dutch speakers become comfortable with compound numbers through daily repetition. Children learn to count from age 2--3, so "vierentwintig" feels as natural to them as "twenty-four" to English speakers. When you hear natives say numbers, they don't pause or reverse-translate; they just say the Dutch word.

Additionally, in the Netherlands, payment is heavily digital. Most shops accept "pin" (debit card), and contactless payments (via chip or phone) are extremely common. While cash is still accepted in most places, learners should be prepared to hear "Pin, alstublieft" (Card, please) more often than "contant" (cash) in modern Dutch cities.

Prices and the Comma ​

Dutch uses a comma (,) for decimal separators, not a period (.). So €4,50 is read "vier euro vijftig" (four euros fifty) or "vier euro en vijftig cent" (four euros and fifty cents). This is different from English, which uses a period. When speaking prices, learners almost always omit "cent" and just say the short form: "vier vijftig" instead of "vier euro vijftig cent."

Memory Tricks ​

Trick 1: "Teens" Are Thirteen Through Nineteen ​

Numbers 13--19 all end in "-tien":

  • dertien, viertien, vijftien, zestien, zeventien, achttien, negentien

Memorize these as a block, and the rest of the numbers become predictable.

Trick 2: Compound Numbers Follow a Pattern ​

After 20, every number from 21--99 follows the formula: unit + en + tens

Once you know the units (1--9) and tens (30, 40, 50...), you can build any number:

  • 4 (vier) + en + 20 (twintig) = vierentwintig
  • 5 (vijf) + en + 30 (dertig) = vijfendertig
  • 7 (zeven) + en + 40 (veertig) = zevenenveertig

Trick 3: Ordinals Follow Patterns Too ​

Most ordinals (4--19) use "-de":

  • vierde, vijfde, zesde, zevende, negende

The exceptions are:

  • eerste (first)
  • tweede (second)
  • derde (third)
  • achtste (eighth -- uses "-ste" instead of "-de")

From 20 onwards, all use "-ste": twintigste, eenentwintigste, etc.

Trick 4: Plurals Are Mostly --en or --s ​

Most Dutch plurals use "-en":

  • boek → boeken
  • kat → katten
  • huis → huizen

A smaller group uses "-s":

  • tafel → tafels
  • kamer → kamers

Irregular plurals (kind → kinderen, ei → eieren) must be memorized individually.

Trick 5: Link "Hoeveel" to "How Many" ​

"Hoeveel" contains "veel" (much/many). Think of it as "How-many/much?" This reinforces its meaning and helps you remember the word.

  7. "Hoeveel kost het?" = "How much does it cost?" Answer: "Dit kost vier euro." (This costs four euros.)

  8. Vijf euro vijftig. (or: Vijf euro en vijftig cent, but the short form is more common.)

  9. A back-of-throat guttural sound (like "kh" in German "Bach"). This is distinctly Dutch and differs from English "ch," so accurate pronunciation helps you sound more native-like.

  10. If you have "één boek" (one book -- singular), then the question should be "Hoeveel boeken heb je?" (How many books do you have?) only works if you have 2+. The question contains a logical error if you only have one.

Summary ​

What You've Learned ​

By finishing this lesson, you now know:

✓ Cardinal numbers 0--100: You can count fluently and understand Dutch numbers in context. ✓ Compound number structure: You understand why Dutch reverses numbers 21--99 and can say them confidently. ✓ "Hoeveel?" questions: You can ask "How many?" and "How much?" with confidence and recognize the question word in fast speech. ✓ Plural nouns with numbers: You know that any noun after a number > 1 must be plural. ✓ Ordinal numbers 1st--10th: You can say "first," "second," "third," etc., and use them in sentences. ✓ Basic monetary expressions: You understand prices in euros and cents and can discuss payment methods. ✓ Number--noun agreement: You know that numbers don't change; nouns do.

Key Takeaways ​

  • Numbers are foundational to daily Dutch. You'll hear them constantly.
  • The reversed order (unit + en + tens) feels strange at first, but it becomes automatic with repetition.
  • All plural nouns use "de," even if their singular form is "het."
  • Dutch is heavily digital in payment; "pin" (debit card) and "contactloos" (contactless) are more common than cash.
