A0-05 Research Output — Counting and Basic Quantities

Scope: Netherlands Dutch, A0 level, balanced across shopping, quantities, age, dates, and everyday number use. Evidence checked against public Dutch-learning and Netherlands payment/currency sources. Dutch numbers use a “reversed” pattern from 21–99: unit + en + ten, e.g., eenentwintig = one-and-twenty. Dutch plurals commonly use -en or -s, and plural nouns always take de as the definite article. In the Netherlands, everyday payment language should include PIN/card/contactless because debit-card and contactless payments dominate point-of-sale usage, while cash is still accepted in most places.

Grammar Concepts
1. Cardinal Numbers 0–100

Explanation
 Dutch cardinal numbers are the basic counting words: nul, één, twee, drie… honderd. Numbers 0–20 must be learned individually. Numbers 30–90 are tens forms ending mostly in -tig: dertig, veertig, vijftig, zestig, zeventig, tachtig, negentig. From 21–99, Dutch places the unit before the ten: vierendertig = four-and-thirty = 34.

Pronunciation focus

g/ch in acht, tachtig, negentig is a rough throat sound.
In compounds with vowel collision, written Dutch may use a diaeresis: tweeëndertig, drieënveertig; it shows vowels are pronounced separately.
Stress usually falls clearly on the first meaningful number part in teaching pronunciation: VIJF-en-twin-tig, DRIE-en-der-tig.

Why it matters
 Learners need numbers for prices, age, addresses, phone numbers, dates, classroom tasks, quantities, and shopping.

Examples

Ik heb vijf boeken. — I have five books.
Dit kost tien euro. — This costs ten euros.
Ik ben vijfentwintig jaar. — I am twenty-five years old.
Nummer vierendertig. — Number thirty-four.
Er zijn honderd appels. — There are one hundred apples.

Common mistakes

Saying twintig-vier instead of vierentwintig.
Forgetting en in compound numbers.
Mispronouncing acht/tachtig without the Dutch throat sound.
Confusing een “a/an” with één “one”.

Builds on A0-02/A0-03
 Uses known structures ik heb, dit is, dit kost, er zijn, and demonstratives such as dit/dat from earlier lessons.

2. “Hoeveel?” Question Formation

Explanation
 Hoeveel? means How many? / How much? It is a WH-question word. In Dutch WH-questions, the conjugated verb comes directly after the question word:

Hoeveel appels heb je? — How many apples do you have?
Hoeveel kost het? — How much does it cost?

Compare with yes/no questions, where the verb comes first:

Heb je vijf appels? — Do you have five apples?
Kost het vijf euro? — Does it cost five euros?

Why it matters
 This is the core question for quantities and prices.

Examples

Hoeveel katten heb je? — How many cats do you have?
Ik heb twee katten. — I have two cats.
Hoeveel kost dit? — How much does this cost?
Dit kost vier euro. — This costs four euros.
Hoeveel boeken zijn er? — How many books are there?

Common mistakes

English word order: Hoeveel je hebt appels?
Using hoe veel as two words in standard writing; hoeveel is one word.
Answering with only English-style noun singular: twee appel instead of twee appels.

Builds on earlier lessons
 Learners already know hebben/zijn and simple questions. This adds a WH-word and quantity meaning.

3. Ordinal Numbers

Explanation
 Ordinal numbers show order: first, second, third. A0 should master 1st–10th and recognize the pattern:

eerste, tweede, derde are high-frequency forms.
Most 4–19 use -de: vierde, vijfde, zesde.
achtste uses -ste.
From 20+, use -ste: twintigste, eenentwintigste.

Why it matters
 Ordinals appear in floors, dates, rankings, steps, and classroom sequences.

Examples

De eerste appel is rood. — The first apple is red.
Ik woon op de derde verdieping. — I live on the third floor.
Dit is de tweede les. — This is the second lesson.
Hij is eerste. — He is first.
Vandaag is de tiende. — Today is the tenth.

Common mistakes

Saying eenste instead of eerste.
Saying driede instead of derde.
Saying achtde instead of achtste.
Using cardinals for sequence: de drie les instead of de derde les.

Builds on earlier lessons
 Uses known demonstratives and nouns: dit is, de les, de man/vrouw, mijn.

4. Plural Noun Forms in Counting Contexts

Explanation
 After numbers above one, Dutch normally uses a plural noun: twee appels, vijf katten, drie boeken. Dutch plurals commonly end in -en or -s. All plural nouns use de as the definite article, even if singular is het: het boek → de boeken.

Basic patterns:

-en: boek → boeken, kat → katten
-s: tafel → tafels, kamer → kamers
Irregular: kind → kinderen, ei → eieren

Why it matters
 Counting requires plural nouns constantly.

Examples

één appel, twee appels — one apple, two apples
één kat, drie katten — one cat, three cats
het boek, de boeken — the book, the books
één kind, twee kinderen — one child, two children
vijf tafels — five tables

Common mistakes

Forgetting plural after numbers: twee boek.
Applying English -s everywhere: boeks instead of boeken.
Forgetting article change: het boeken instead of de boeken.

Builds on earlier lessons
 Learners know nouns/articles from descriptive vocabulary; this introduces how nouns change when counted.

5. Number–Noun Agreement

Explanation
 Dutch numbers themselves do not change for gender or number. The noun changes:

één boek — one book
twee boeken — two books
één kat — one cat
drie katten — three cats

Use één when emphasizing the number “one”; use een for “a/an” or unstressed “one”.

Why it matters
 Learners need to combine numbers with objects naturally.

Examples

Ik heb één boek. — I have one book.
Ik heb twee boeken. — I have two books.
Dat zijn drie katten. — Those are three cats.
Er is één huis. — There is one house.
Er zijn vier huizen. — There are four houses.

Common mistakes

Adding plural to number: twees boeken.
Using singular noun after numbers above one.
Confusing is and zijn: er is één, er zijn twee.

Builds on earlier lessons
 Extends zijn/hebben into number contexts.

6. Basic Monetary Expressions

Explanation
 The Netherlands uses the euro. One euro is divided into 100 cent; euro notes and coins are used in the euro area, and common coins include 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 cent and €1/€2 denominations. Prices are written with a comma as decimal separator: €4,50. Read simply as vier euro vijftig or vier euro en vijftig cent.

Core forms:

Hoeveel kost het? — How much does it cost?
Dit kost vijf euro. — This costs five euros.
De prijs is tien euro. — The price is ten euros.
Ik betaal met pin. — I pay by card/PIN.

Why it matters
 A0 learners need numbers for shopping and cafés. Dutch point-of-sale payments are largely debit-card/contactless: the Dutch Payments Association reports debit cards and cash as major POS methods, with contactless payments at 94% by end-2024.

Examples

Hoeveel kost dit brood? — How much does this bread cost?
Dit kost drie euro. — This costs three euros.
Dat is vier euro vijftig. — That is four euros fifty.
Ik betaal met pin. — I pay by card.
Heb je wisselgeld? — Do you have change?

Common mistakes

Saying dollars instead of euro in Dutch context.
Reading 4,50 as “four point fifty” instead of vier euro vijftig.
Overusing cash language in the Netherlands, where PIN/card is very common.

Builds on earlier lessons
 Uses dit/dat, het is, ik heb, ik betaal, and number vocabulary.

Cultural Notes — Netherlands Context
Currency and written prices

The Netherlands uses the euro, divided into 100 cent. Written prices use a comma as decimal separator: €4,50, not €4.50. In speech, Dutch often shortens prices: vier vijftig or vier euro vijftig.

Everyday prices

For realistic A0 references, use simple ranges rather than exact claims: supermarket bread can be around a few euros, coffee in cafés often a few euros, and inexpensive meals commonly start around the low tens. For current cost examples, Numbeo’s July 2026 Netherlands data lists a regular cappuccino at €3.73, an inexpensive restaurant meal at €20.00, and a fresh white bread loaf at €1.85. Treat these as approximate lesson anchors, not fixed prices.

Payment methods

Dutch learners should know pin, met pin betalen, and contactloos. The Dutch Payments Association says the Dutch mostly pay with debit cards at points of sale, and contactless payments reached 94% of debit/credit card payments by end-2024. DNB’s 2026 cash-acceptance survey found only 4% of visited retailers had a clear PIN only sticker, so learners may still encounter both cash and card options.

Number use in daily life

Numbers appear in:

Phone numbers: often read in groups; Dutch mobile numbers usually begin with 06, often spoken as nul zes.
Addresses: huisnummer follows the street name, e.g., Kerkstraat 12.
Postal codes: four digits + two letters, e.g., 1234 AB, read as digits plus letters.
Public transport: platform, tram, bus, and train numbers.
Ordinals in daily Dutch life

Ordinals appear in floors (de derde verdieping), lists (de eerste vraag), rankings (hij is eerste), and dates (de tiende).

Pluralization vs English

English often adds -s; Dutch alternates between -en and -s, with spelling changes and irregulars. Also, plural nouns always use de: het huis → de huizen.

Assessment Criteria
Counting standard

Learner can count aloud 0–100, including tens and compound numbers, with understandable pronunciation and correct reversed structure for 21–99.

Listening/comprehension standard

Learner can hear common numbers, prices, ages, and quantities and write the correct digit or choose the correct object/price.

Speaking standard

Learner can ask and answer:

Hoeveel appels heb je?
Hoeveel kost het?
Hoe oud ben je?
Usage-in-context standard

Learner can use numbers with plural nouns, simple quantities, prices in euros/cents, and ordinal numbers 1st–10th in classroom or daily contexts.

Sample assessment tasks
Oral counting: Count from 0 to 30, then by tens to 100.
Listening: Hear vierenveertig, €4,50, de derde, and write the correct forms.
Speaking role-play: Ask the price of bread and pay by card: Hoeveel kost dit brood? Ik betaal met pin.
Writing: Convert digits to words: 5, 12, 21, 34, 50, 99.
