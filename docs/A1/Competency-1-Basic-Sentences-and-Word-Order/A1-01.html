Lesson A1-01: Basic Sentences and Word Order ​

Level: A1 (Beginner) CEFR Level: A1 Duration: 90 minutes New Vocabulary: 32 words Grammar Focus: Present tense verb conjugation, subject-verb agreement, SVO word order, yes/no questions via inversion, question-word questions, negation with "niet"

1. Lesson Goal ​

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to construct simple present-tense sentences with correct subject-verb agreement, form yes/no questions using verb inversion, ask question-word questions (who, what, where, when, why, how), and place negation correctly in basic Dutch sentences.

2. Previous Knowledge ​

Before starting this lesson, you should have completed:

  • A0-01: Understanding Dutch Sounds and Spelling
  • A0-02: Introducing Yourself (personal pronouns: ik, jij, u, hij, zij, het, wij, jullie, zij)
  • A0-03: Describing People and Family
  • A0-04: Greeting and Basic Politeness
  • A0-05: Counting and Basic Quantities
  • A0-06: Telling Time and Dates

You should be comfortable with:

  • Pronouncing Dutch sounds and vowel patterns
  • Recognizing personal pronouns and their functions
  • Understanding basic be-verbs (zijn) and have-verbs (hebben) from A0

3. Why This Matters ​

Present-tense verb conjugation and word order are the foundation of productive Dutch. In A0, you learned chunks: "ik ben," "jij bent," "hij is." Now, you'll turn those patterns into a productive rule you can apply to any verb. This lesson lets you take vocabulary you already know and build new sentences with it. You can tell people what you do, ask what they do, and describe daily routines -- the core of beginner conversation.

4. Grammar ​

4.1 Present Tense Verb Conjugation Review ​

For most regular Dutch verbs, the pattern is simple: start from the infinitive ending in -en, remove -en, adjust spelling if needed, then add the correct present-tense ending based on the subject.

SubjectPatternExample (luisteren)English
ikstemIk luister.I listen.
jij / jestem + -tJij luistert.You listen.
ustem + -tU luistert.You (formal) listen.
hijstem + -tHij luistert.He listens.
zijstem + -tZij luistert.She listens.
hetstem + -tHet luistert niet.It does not listen.
wij / weinfinitiveWij luisteren.We listen.
jullieinfinitiveJullie luisteren.You (plural) listen.
zij / zeinfinitiveZij luisteren.They listen.

Note on spelling: Some verbs require spelling changes to preserve Dutch sound patterns. For example, lezen (to read) → ik lees (I read), not "ik lez." These follow predictable rules based on Dutch phonetics.

Common present-tense shapes:

  • Stem: ik werk, ik luister
  • Stem + -t: hij werkt, jij luistert, u werkt
  • Infinitive: wij werken, zij luisteren, jullie lezen

Why it matters: This turns memorized A0 chunks into productive sentence building. You can take a new verb like "lezen" and automatically produce ik lees, jij leest, hij leest, wij lezen, etc.

See: /shared/grammar/present-tense-conjugation.md for additional details and irregular verbs.

4.2 Subject-Verb Agreement in Context ​

The verb form must always match the subject pronoun. This agreement is what makes Dutch sentences sound "finished" and fluent.

Dutch has three main present-tense verb shapes:

ShapePronounsExamples
stemikIk werk. Ik lees. Ik schrijf.
stem + -tjij/u/hij/zij/hetJij werkt. U werkt. Hij werkt. Zij werkt. Het werkt niet.
infinitivewij/jullie/zij (plural)Wij werken. Jullie lezen. Zij schrijven.

Common mistakes:

  • Ik werken → Ik werk (ik does not take the infinitive)
  • Hij werk → Hij werkt (third-person singular needs -t)
  • Wij werkt → Wij werken (plural forms use the infinitive)

Also note: A singular noun like "de man" or "het kind" behaves like hij/zij/het and takes stem + -t:

  • De man werkt. (The man works.)
  • Het kind luistert. (The child listens.)

A plural noun like "de mannen" or "de kinderen" behaves like zij (plural) and takes the infinitive:

  • De mannen werken. (The men work.)
  • De kinderen luisteren. (The children listen.)

4.3 SVO Word Order in Main Clauses ​

Dutch main clauses typically follow Subject--Verb--Object/Rest (SVO) order, just like English:

  • Ik lees een boek. (I read a book.)
  • Jij leert Nederlands. (You learn Dutch.)
  • Wij kijken naar het raam. (We look at the window.)

The deeper Dutch rule is called Verb Second (V2): the finite verb must be the second element in the main clause. If a time/place word or other adverbial starts the sentence, the verb still comes second:

  • Nu lees ik. (Now read I. / Now I read.)
  • Vandaag werkt hij thuis. (Today works he at-home. / Today he works at home.)
  • Daarna schrijven wij. (After-that write we. / After that we write.)

Why it matters: Learners can begin with reliable A1 frames (subject-first SVO) before later learning longer subordinate clauses where the verb moves toward the end.

Common mistakes:

  • English-style fronting without inversion: Vandaag ik werk → Vandaag werk ik.
  • Moving the main verb to the end in simple sentences.
  • Confusing main clauses (V2 order) with subordinate clauses (verb-final, taught in A2+).

4.4 Yes/No Questions via Verb Inversion ​

To form a yes/no question in Dutch, invert the subject and finite verb: Verb + subject + rest.

StatementQuestionEnglish
Jij spreekt Nederlands.Spreek jij Nederlands?Do you speak Dutch?
Hij komt vandaag.Komt hij vandaag?Is he coming today?
Wij lezen nu.Lezen wij nu?Are we reading now?
U werkt hier.Werkt u hier?Do you work here?

Important: There is no Dutch "do/does" helper verb for ordinary yes/no questions. Just invert the verb and subject.

The jij -t drop: When jij comes directly after the verb, the -t ending falls away:

  • Jij werkt. (You work.) → Werk jij? (Do you work?)
  • Jij luistert. (You listen.) → Luister jij? (Are you listening?)
  • Jij schrijft. (You write.) → Schrijf jij? (Are you writing?)

This happens automatically in speech and writing; it's a standard feature of Dutch grammar.

Common mistakes:

  • Adding English "do": Doe jij spreken? → Spreek jij?
  • Keeping statement order as the neutral question: Jij spreekt Nederlands? (This can be a checking question in casual speech, but the standard form is Spreek jij Nederlands?)
  • Forgetting the jij -t drop: Werkt jij? instead of Werk jij?

4.5 Question-Word Questions ​

To ask who, what, where, when, why, or how, use the pattern: Question word + finite verb + subject + rest.

Core question words:

  • wie (who) -- Wie ben jij?
  • wat (what) -- Wat lees je?
  • waar (where) -- Waar woon je?
  • wanneer (when) -- Wanneer begin je?
  • waarom (why) -- Waarom kijk je?
  • hoe (how) -- Hoe gaat het?
  • hoeveel (how much/many) -- Hoeveel kinderen heb je?

Examples:

  • Wie ben jij? (Who are you?)
  • Wat lees je? (What are you reading?)
  • Waar woon je? (Where do you live?)
  • Wanneer begin je? (When do you start?)
  • Hoe gaat het? (How are you? / How is it going?)
  • Hoeveel heb je? (How many do you have?)

Common mistakes:

  • Waar je woont? (Dutch word order: Waar woon je?)
  • Adding "do": Wat doe je lezen? → Wat lees je?
  • Forgetting that hoeveel is one word, not two.
  • Overusing wat where wie is needed for people: Wat is je naam? (incorrect) → Wie is je naam? (still wrong) → What is your name? can be Wat is je naam? if asking for the string; more naturally, just Wie ben jij? if asking who they are.

4.6 Negation with "niet" ​

Dutch uses niet (not) for verbs, adjectives/descriptions, definite/specific nouns, and most whole statements. It uses geen (not a / not any) before indefinite nouns.

For A1, learn these dependable niet positions:

  1. At or near the end in short sentences: Ik werk niet. (I do not work.)
  2. Before adjectives/descriptions: Het is niet goed. (It is not good.)
  3. Before places: Ik woon niet in Amsterdam. (I do not live in Amsterdam.)
  4. After a definite object: Ik begrijp de vraag niet. (I do not understand the question.)
  5. Before a final infinitive: Ik kan niet komen. (I cannot come.) / Ik wil niet leren. (I do not want to learn.)
  6. After a time expression: Hij komt vandaag niet. (He is not coming today.)

For "geen" (indefinite nouns):

  • Ik heb een boek. (I have a book.) → Ik heb geen boek. (I do not have a book.)
  • Ik drink koffie. (I drink coffee.) → Ik drink geen koffie. (I do not drink coffee.) [mass noun, treated as indefinite]

Common mistakes:

  • Ik niet werk → Ik werk niet.
  • Ik heb niet een boek → Ik heb geen boek.
  • Het is goed niet → Het is niet goed.
  • Using niet before an infinitive at the start: Niet gaan ik → Ik ga niet. (or Niet gaan. as a command, but not in a standard statement)

5. Grammar Reasoning ​

Why does Dutch present-tense conjugation work this way?

Dutch conjugation is simpler than it looks. The pattern ik + stem, hij + stem + -t, wij + infinitive comes from the history of Germanic languages. The -t ending for third-person singular (hij, zij, het) is a remnant of Old English -eth (he worketh). The infinitive for plurals (wij, jullie, zij) reflects the fact that older forms didn't distinguish person in plural conjugation.

For English speakers, this is intuitive: you hear the same stem-based patterns in English ("I work," "he works," "we work"), just with fewer endings and clearer rules.

Why V2 word order (verb-second)?

Dutch, German, and Old English all use Verb-Second (V2) in main clauses. It's a deep structural feature. When you start a sentence with a time or place ("Vandaag ..."), the verb must still be second. This isn't random; it's tied to how Dutch processes information: the topic comes first, then the verb, then the new information. It forces clarity.

Why inversion in questions?

Inversion (verb before subject) marks a question as a genuine request for information, not a statement or assumption. In English, we use "do/does," but Dutch simply moves the verb forward. This is more economical.

Why "niet" is flexible:

Dutch "niet" is flexible because it can apply to different parts of the sentence. "Ik werk niet" negates the entire action. "Ik werk niet in Amsterdam" negates the location. "Het is niet goed" negates the quality. This flexibility mirrors how speakers naturally emphasize what they're negating. English does something similar with stress: "I don't WORK in Amsterdam" vs. "I don't work IN AMSTERDAM," but Dutch marks this more explicitly with word placement.

7. Pronunciation ​

Question words ​

  • wie (VEE) -- Like the English word "we"
  • wat (VAHT) -- Like "vat" (container), but Dutch /a/ is flatter
  • waar (VAHR) -- Like "var" with the Dutch /a/ and a slight roll or uvular R
  • wanneer (vahn-NEER) -- Like "vahn" + "near"
  • waarom (vah-ROHM) -- Like "vah" + "rohm" (with guttural Dutch R)
  • hoe (HOO) -- Like "hoo" in "hooray"
  • hoeveel (hoo-VAYL) -- "hoo" + "vayl" (rhymes with "veil")

Core verbs (infinitives, then example conjugation) ​

  • luisteren (LOW-stuh-run) -- ik luister, hij luistert, wij luisteren
  • lezen (LAY-zun) -- ik lees (note the double e), hij leest, wij lezen
  • schrijven (SKHRY-vun) -- ik schrijf, hij schrijft, wij schrijven
  • kijken (KY-kun) -- ik kijk, hij kijkt, wij kijken
  • denken (DEN-kun) -- ik denk, hij denkt, wij denken
  • voelen (VOO-lun) -- ik voel, hij voelt, wij voelen
  • zien (ZEEN) -- ik zie, hij ziet, wij zien
  • horen (HOH-run) -- ik hoor, hij hoort, wij horen
  • willen (WIL-un) -- ik wil, hij wil, wij willen (Note: no -t in hij form for irregular verbs like willen)
  • kunnen (KUH-nun) -- ik kan, hij kan, wij kunnen (irregular)
  • moeten (MOO-tun) -- ik moet, hij moet, wij moeten (irregular)
  • mogen (MOH-ghun) -- ik mag, hij mag, wij mogen (irregular)

Adverbs and connectors ​

  • altijd (AHL-tayt) -- "always," stress on first syllable
  • soms (SOMS) -- "sometimes," short and crisp
  • nooit (NOYT) -- "never," like English "nite" but with Dutch vowel
  • nu (NUU) -- "now," rounded lips, like French "oo"
  • misschien (mis-SKHEEN) -- "maybe," three syllables
  • maar (MAHR) -- "but," like "car" with Dutch /a/
  • dus (DUS) -- "so," like "bus" with Dutch /u/

8. Dialogue ​

Dialogue 1: Meeting Someone ​

Dutch:

A: Hoi, wie ben jij? B: Ik ben Sara. Spreek jij Nederlands? A: Ja, ik spreek een beetje Nederlands. B: Goed! Wat doe jij? A: Ik ben leraar. En jij? B: Ik ben arts.

English Translation:

A: Hi, who are you? B: I'm Sara. Do you speak Dutch? A: Yes, I speak a little Dutch. B: Good! What do you do? A: I'm a teacher. And you? B: I'm a doctor.

Grammar notes: Wie ben jij? uses question word + verb + subject; Spreek jij? shows yes/no inversion with the jij -t drop (from "Jij spreekt" → "Spreek jij").

Dialogue 2: At Home ​

Dutch:

A: Lees jij nu? B: Nee, ik lees niet. Ik schrijf. A: Wat schrijf je? B: Ik schrijf een email. A: Aan wie? B: Aan mijn moeder.

English Translation:

A: Are you reading now? B: No, I'm not reading. I'm writing. A: What are you writing? B: I'm writing an email. A: To whom? B: To my mother.

Grammar notes: Lees jij? is verb-first (yes/no question); "ik lees niet" places niet at the end; "Aan wie?" is a question-word question.

Dialogue 3: Daily Routine ​

Dutch:

A: Wanneer begin je vandaag? B: Ik begin om negen uur. A: Werk je altijd thuis? B: Nee, ik werk soms thuis. A: Hoe gaat het? B: Het gaat goed, dank je.

English Translation:

A: When do you start today? B: I start at nine o'clock. A: Do you always work at home? B: No, I sometimes work at home. A: How are you? B: I'm doing well, thanks.

Grammar notes: Question-word question keeps the verb second; "ik werk soms thuis" shows adverb placement (time/manner adverbs come after the verb in simple sentences); "Hoe gaat het?" is a fixed phrase.

Dialogue 4: Plans ​

Dutch:

A: Kunnen wij nu gaan? B: Nee, wij kunnen niet gaan. A: Waarom niet? B: Het is nog niet klaar. A: Wanneer is het klaar? B: Misschien morgen.

English Translation:

A: Can we go now? B: No, we cannot go. A: Why not? B: It is not ready yet. A: When will it be ready? B: Maybe tomorrow.

Grammar notes: With two verbs (kunnen + gaan), "niet" comes before the final infinitive: "niet gaan." "Klaar" (ready) is an adjective; "Waarom niet?" is a shortened question (full form: "Waarom kunnen wij niet gaan?").

9. Reading ​

Short Text: A Day at the Office ​

Ik ben Marc. Ik begin mijn dag om acht uur. Ik lees emails en ik schrijf rapporten. Om twaalf uur eet ik lunch. Na lunch luister ik naar meetings.

Mijn collega Sophie werkt ook hier. Zij luistert naar de meetings ook. Om vijf uur gaan we naar huis. Sophie woont dicht bij mij. Wij gaan altijd samen naar huis.

On Wednesday evening I watch football (soccer). I like to watch football. My friends watch too. We never watch football alone.

10. Listening ​

Transcript: Interview at a Café ​

Comprehension Scenario: You overhear two people talking at a café. Listen to (or read) the conversation and answer the questions.

Dialogue:

A: Hallo, ik ben Linda. Wie ben jij? B: Ik ben Tom. Aangenaam! A: Aangenaam! Tom, wat doe jij? B: Ik ben programmeur. En jij? A: Ik ben grafica. Werk je hier in Amsterdam? B: Ja, ik werk hier. Jij ook? A: Nee, ik werk in Rotterdam. Maar ik kom hier op woensdag. B: Waarom kom je op woensdag? A: Mijn zus woont hier. Ik eet altijd met haar op woensdag. B: Dat is leuk! Hoe lang ken je je zus al? A: Heel lang -- wij zijn zusjes sinds we klein waren.

English Translation ​

A: Hello, I'm Linda. Who are you? B: I'm Tom. Pleased to meet you! A: Pleased to meet you! Tom, what do you do? B: I'm a programmer. And you? A: I'm a graphic designer. Do you work here in Amsterdam? B: Yes, I work here. You too? A: No, I work in Rotterdam. But I come here on Wednesday. B: Why do you come on Wednesday? A: My sister lives here. I always eat with her on Wednesday. B: That's nice! How long have you known your sister? A: A long time -- we are sisters since we were small.

Comprehension Questions ​

  1. Wat doet Tom? (What does Tom do?)
  2. Waar werkt Linda? (Where does Linda work?)
  3. Waarom komt Linda op woensdag? (Why does Linda come on Wednesday?)
  4. Eet Linda altijd met haar zus? (Does Linda always eat with her sister?)
  5. Werkt Linda ook hier in Amsterdam? (Does Linda work here in Amsterdam too?)

Answer Key ​

  1. Tom is a programmer. / Hij is programmeur.
  2. Linda works in Rotterdam. / Zij werkt in Rotterdam.
  3. Because her sister lives here and they eat together. / Haar zus woont hier en zij eten op woensdag.
  4. Yes. / Ja.
  5. No, she works in Rotterdam. / Nee, zij werkt in Rotterdam.

13. Common Mistakes ​

Mistake 1: Wrong Verb Form for the Subject ​

Wrong: Ik werken. / Wij werkt. Right: Ik werk. / Wij werken. Why: "Ik" always takes the stem (no ending). Plural subjects (wij, jullie, zij) always take the infinitive form (ending in -en).

Mistake 2: Forgetting the -t for Third-Person Singular ​

Wrong: Hij werk. / Zij kijk. Right: Hij werkt. / Zij kijkt. Why: Any singular subject (hij, zij, het) or a singular noun takes stem + -t.

Mistake 3: Keeping the -t in Yes/No Questions with Jij ​

Wrong: Werkt jij? / Luistert jij? Right: Werk jij? / Luister jij? Why: When jij comes directly after the verb in a question, the -t ending drops. This is automatic in Dutch.

Mistake 4: English-Style Fronting Without Inversion ​

Wrong: Vandaag ik werk. / Nu jij schrijf? Right: Vandaag werk ik. / Nu jij schrijft? (statement, checking; or: Schrijf jij nu?) Why: Dutch V2 rule: the finite verb must be the second element in the main clause. Fronting a time/place word requires inversion.

Mistake 5: Misplacing "niet" or Using it Instead of "geen" ​

Wrong: Ik niet werk. / Ik heb niet een boek. Right: Ik werk niet. / Ik heb geen boek. Why: "Niet" comes after the verb or before specific elements being negated. "Geen" is for indefinite nouns (boek = a book, books, or mass nouns).

Mistake 6: Adding English "do/does" to Questions ​

Wrong: Doe jij spreken Nederlands? / Wat doe je lezen? Right: Spreek jij Nederlands? / Wat lees je? Why: Dutch has no auxiliary "do" for questions. The verb alone is inverted.

Mistake 7: Confusing Singular Noun Agreement with Verb Forms ​

Wrong: De man werken. / De kinderen werkt. Right: De man werkt. (singular noun = hij/zij form) / De kinderen werken. (plural noun = zij form) Why: A singular noun (even if it's "de man," not a personal pronoun) triggers stem + -t. A plural noun triggers the infinitive, just like plural pronouns.

14. Dutch Insight ​

Dutch main clauses feel friendly to English speakers because subject-first sentences are often SVO, but the stronger Dutch principle is Verb Second (V2): the finite verb must stay second in main clauses, even after time/place openers.

French and Spanish also use SVO often, but Dutch takes a different structural approach than those Romance languages. German subordinate clauses send the finite verb to the end, similar to later Dutch subordinate clauses (taught in A2+); A1 learners should first master simple Dutch main clauses and questions.

Dutch questions are direct. "Kom je?" "Waar woon je?" "Wat wil je?" This directness fits a broader communication style in which Dutch speakers often value clarity, frankness, and short, straightforward phrasing. You should not over-soften every question; polite tone, "alsjeblieft" (please), and context matter, but grammatical directness is normal and expected.

Variation is mainly about address and tone, not the core question pattern. "U" is formal/polite; "jij/je" is common informal address. Statement-shaped questions such as "Je komt morgen?" can occur when checking an assumption, but the neutral, standard A1 production target is inversion: "Kom je morgen?"

15. Memory Tricks ​

Trick 1: The Three Shapes Rule ​

Dutch present-tense verbs have exactly three shapes:

  • ik = stem (Ik werk, ik lees)
  • hij/zij/het/jij/u = stem + -t (He works, she works, you work, you work formal)
  • wij/jullie/zij (they) = infinitive (We work, you work plural, they work)

Think of it as a pyramid: ik at the top (simplest form), a big middle layer (stem + -t for singular formal/third-person), and the base (infinitive for all plurals).

Trick 2: Verb Inversion = Question ​

To convert a statement to a yes/no question, simply flip the verb and subject.

  • Statement: Jij werkt.
  • Question: Werk jij?

The verb "flips" to the front, and subject follows.

Trick 3: The jij -t Drop ​

When you form a yes/no question with "jij," the -t always falls away:

  • Jij werkt → Werk jij?
  • Jij luistert → Luister jij?
  • Jij schrijft → Schrijf jij?

Mnemonic: "jij jumps to the front" and "loses its tail (-t)" in the flip.

Trick 4: "Niet" at the End for Simple Negation ​

If you're unsure where to put "niet," try the end of the sentence first:

  • Ik work. → Ik work niet.
  • Jij lees. → Jij lees niet.

This works for most short, simple sentences.

Trick 5: "Geen" for Indefinite Things ​

If you're negating something that could be replaced with "a" (een) or a bare plural/mass noun, use "geen":

  • Ik heb een boek. → Ik heb geen boek. (not a book)
  • Ik drink koffie. → Ik drink geen koffie. (not coffee, no coffee)

If it already has "the" (de/het) and is specific, use "niet":

  • Ik lees de krant. → Ik lees de krant niet. (I don't read the newspaper / the specific newspaper is not being read)

Answer Key ​

  1. Three forms: (1) stem for ik, (2) stem + -t for jij/u/hij/zij/het, (3) infinitive for wij/jullie/zij (plural).

  2. Form a yes/no question: Invert the subject and finite verb. Verb comes first, subject second: "Jij werkt" → "Werkt jij?"

  3. Why the jij -t drops: When "jij" follows the verb directly in a question, the -t is dropped as part of Dutch phonology and word-order rules. It's a standard feature. (Explanation: The -t was originally a marker to distinguish jij in statement form, but in questions, the position of jij already makes the person clear, so the -t becomes redundant and is dropped.)

  4. Where "niet" goes: In simple sentences, "niet" typically goes near or at the end: "Ik werk niet." It can also come before the specific element being negated, like "Ik werk niet thuis" (I don't work at home, specifically not the location).

  5. "Niet" vs "geen":

    • "Niet" negates verbs, adjectives, specific/definite nouns, places, and often whole statements.
    • "Geen" negates indefinite nouns (those that could have "een" or a bare plural/mass noun).

  6. Five question words:

    • wie (who)
    • wat (what)
    • waar (where)
    • wanneer (when)
    • waarom (why)
    • hoe (how)
    • hoeveel (how much/many)

  7. V2 rule: The finite verb must be the second element in a main clause. Even if you front a time/place adverbial, the verb stays second: "Vandaag werk ik" (Today work I / Today I work). It matters because it's a core structural principle of Dutch and forces specific word order.

  8. Infinitive "lezen" → hij form:

    • Infinitive: lezen
    • Stem: lez- (remove -en)
    • Stem becomes: lees (spelling adjustment to keep the long vowel sound)
    • Add -t: leest (hij leest / she reads)

  9. "Ik heb een pen": Use "geen" because "een pen" is indefinite. Answer: "Ik heb geen pen" (I don't have a pen).

  10. True or False: False. Dutch does NOT use "do/does" in yes/no questions. It simply inverts the verb and subject.

19. Summary ​

Key Takeaways ​

Dutch present-tense verb conjugation has three simple shapes:

  • ik + stem (Ik werk, ik lees)
  • hij/zij/het/jij/u + stem + -t (He works, she works, you work)
  • wij/jullie/zij (plural) + infinitive (We work, you work plural, they work)

Dutch main clauses follow a Verb-Second (V2) rule: the finite verb must be the second element, even if you front a time or place word: "Vandaag werk ik" (Today work I = Today I work).

Yes/no questions are formed by verb inversion: "Jij werkt" → "Werkt jij?" When jij comes after the verb, the -t drops: "Werk jij?"

Question-word questions use the pattern: Question word + finite verb + subject + rest: "Waar woon jij?" (Where live you? = Where do you live?)

Negation with "niet" or "geen":

  • Use "niet" for verbs, adjectives, definite nouns, and most statements. It usually goes near the end or before the element being negated.
  • Use "geen" for indefinite nouns (those that would take "een" or a bare plural/mass noun).
